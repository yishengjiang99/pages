In Praise of Doing One Thing Badly, Slowly

A field guide to the vanishing art of unhurried work, written over eleven months and three kettles.

By M. Arden · Photographs by nobody · 14 min

There is a particular silence that arrives only after the third hour of a task. It is not the silence of concentration, which is brittle and loud with effort, but something softer, a kind of furniture of the mind settling into place. Most of us never reach it. We leave at the first creak.

The old bookbinders of Leiden had a phrase for this: het werk laten rusten, to let the work rest. A spine glued in the morning was not touched until evening. Not because it was fragile, but because the binder was.

"Speed is a way of not having to look."

Consider the kettle. It will boil at its own pace, indifferent to your schedule, and in the minutes it asks of you there is an invitation: stand there. Watch the window. Let the steam write its brief, illegible letter on the glass.

None of this is efficient. That is rather the point. Efficiency is a measure of distance between intention and outcome, and some of the best things live precisely in that distance, the detours, the wrong turns, the afternoons spent sanding a drawer that was never going to close properly anyway.

So do one thing badly. Do it slowly. Do it again tomorrow, slightly less badly. That, it turns out, is the whole method.
